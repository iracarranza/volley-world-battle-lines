I think these two questions expose the same underlying problem: we have been treating archetypes as destinations in attribute space, when they should instead be outcomes of particular investment paths through that space.

An athlete's POW/ACC ratio cannot, by itself, determine whether they are a Tooler, a Tricky Setter, or a Hybrid Server. Likewise, having exceptional Explosiveness cannot, by itself, make someone a Burst Attacker.

The distinction needs to exist between possessing a capability, specializing in it, and building a playstyle around it.

1. Burst Attacker versus naturally explosive SPD athletes
Your three-athlete example is a strong foundation for this distinction.

Athlete

Source of explosiveness

Relationship to explosiveness

Non-Burst Hitter

Ordinary attribute growth

Does not possess exceptional Explosiveness

Burst Attacker

POW Traits granting exceptional Explosiveness access

Uses unusual explosiveness to create attacking opportunities

SPD Explosiveness Specialist

Deep investment into the Explosiveness subattribute

Is exceptionally explosive across applicable actions

The key is that the Burst Attacker should not simply receive enough Explosiveness to become equivalent to the SPD specialist.

Instead, their Traits should allow them to exploit explosiveness in ways specifically useful to attacking.

For example, a Burst Attacker might eventually acquire techniques that convert sudden approach acceleration into an improved offensive opportunity, or preserve more attack quality after an unusually abrupt approach.

These are illustrative possibilities, not proposed settled mechanics.

The SPD specialist, meanwhile, possesses Explosiveness as a general physical characteristic. They can use it for defensive movement, positioning, approaches, and any other action to which Explosiveness applies.

Crucially, they should also be able to imitate much of the Burst Attacker's behavior if their other capabilities permit it.

I would not artificially prohibit that imitation merely to protect Burst Attacker's identity.

The Burst Attacker's advantage is that their path deliberately assembles an offensive toolkit around a physical quality that is unusual for a POW-focused athlete.

The SPD specialist's advantage is that their explosiveness is inherent to their attribute specialization and useful across a much broader set of circumstances.

Consistency of exceptionality
I think your phrase is especially useful, but I would sharpen its meaning.

The Burst Attacker is not necessarily less consistently explosive in the literal sense. If a Trait grants a persistent Explosiveness improvement, that improvement should behave consistently.

The distinction is how broadly the exception applies.

A Burst Attacker is exceptionally explosive for the kind of athlete they are, and has specific tools to capitalize on it.

A SPD specialist is exceptionally explosive as a general capability, without needing a specialized offensive path to justify that characteristic.

This creates a useful design rule:

Traits may provide exceptional access to a capability, but they should not automatically monopolize the gameplay applications of that capability.

A SPD specialist who also invests in POW could plausibly become an extraordinary explosive hitter without purchasing Burst Attacker's entire path. Whether they choose to do so depends on what the individual Traits actually offer.

2. POW/ACC and ACC/POW should be different
I agree that these should be treated as distinct directions of specialization.

However, I would not define the difference purely through which attribute has more AP invested.

Instead, the ordering should describe which attribute's gameplay identity the path is primarily developing.

POW → ACC

Technical attacker
Starts with offensive threat and develops precision, range, or directional control to improve it.

Examples: Broad Range, Spotter, Tooler

ACC → POW

Threatening technician
Starts with technical ball control and develops enough attacking threat to make that control more dangerous.

Example: Tricky

Tricky as an ACC-primary outcome
Tricky is a particularly good example because its fantasy changes depending on the athlete pursuing it.

A Setter might develop exceptional technical deception, then invest enough POW to make attacking an actual threat. Opponents cannot safely assume that an apparent setting opportunity will result in a set.

A technically oriented hitter might pursue the same path for the opposite practical reason: they already possess credible attacking ability, but want their non-attacking ball interactions to become threatening enough to create uncertainty.

The path is still ACC-primary because its central benefit is deception and technical ambiguity, not improving the effectiveness of the spike itself.

Compare that with Spotter:

Spotter: I am attacking, and I can manipulate the attack's trajectory more effectively.

Tricky: You cannot confidently determine whether I am attacking, setting, or using another available technical option.

Both can benefit from POW and ACC, but they solve different problems.

This also suggests that a player could invest into both, provided they can afford the requirements. They should not be mutually exclusive simply because they occupy opposite attribute directions.

4. Archetypes should be emergent, not exclusive identities
This leads to what I think is the most important structural conclusion.

The athlete does not select an archetype. The athlete selects investments that may collectively produce one or several archetypal outcomes.

Tooler, Burst Attacker, Tricky, and Hybrid Server are design targets, not necessarily formal build classifications enforced by the game.

This distinction resolves several problems simultaneously.

An athlete might be:

A Tooler who has also invested in Hybrid Server.

A Setter who pursues Tricky and then develops Big Server.

A SPD specialist whose exceptional Explosiveness supports a strong attacking game without following the POW Burst Attacker path.

A blocking-focused MB who purchases a small portion of Burst Attacker without committing to its deeper offensive specialization.

These combinations should be allowed where the AP economy permits them.

We should avoid forcing players to choose one named outcome when their actual investments support multiple.

What, then, is the purpose of an archetype?
An archetype provides a target for designing the tree.

It tells us:

Which collection of capabilities should be possible.

What investment burden should be required.

Which positions should naturally find the path attractive.

What exceptional performance or gameplay behavior should reward completing it.

Which other capabilities the athlete is likely to sacrifice to reach it.

It does not necessarily need to be represented as a formal selection in the interface.

5. The remaining question: how much mirroring is desirable?
I would distinguish three kinds of overlap.

Overlap

Example

Design judgment

Capability mirroring

SPD Explosiveness specialist reproduces some Burst Attacker movement and approaches

Desirable

Outcome mirroring

Different POW/ACC and ACC/POW builds both produce threatening technical attackers

Desirable when achieved through meaningfully different investments

Complete path redundancy

Two paths grant the same abilities, efficiencies, and ceilings at similar costs

Generally undesirable

The important issue is not whether two athletes can accomplish the same thing.

It is whether their investments create different strengths, weaknesses, opportunity costs, or ways of achieving it.

For example, a naturally explosive SPD athlete and a Burst Attacker may perform nearly identical approaches in some circumstances. That is acceptable. Their differences become apparent across the rest of the rally and the rest of their build.

Similarly, a Tricky athlete and a Tooler might both execute a technically impressive attack. But Tricky's investment should make deception and alternative ball interactions more effective, while Tooler's investment should maximize scoring through an actual attack.

There is no reason to forbid the shared outcome.

6. A refined model for the entire Trait system
I would now describe the system using four distinct layers:

Attributes

General athletic capability and progression budget
Subattributes

How underlying athletic qualities specialize
Trait paths

Which exceptional techniques and auxiliaries become available
Emergent athlete builds

One or several recognizable playstyle outcomes
The parallel relationship between subattributes and Trait paths is important.

A subattribute specialist may naturally reproduce capabilities associated with a Trait archetype, while a Trait specialist may obtain exceptional access to qualities they would otherwise lack.

Neither system should entirely supersede the other.

One concern I would preserve for later balancing
The risk isn't primarily that Tooler and Hybrid Server might share POW/ACC investment.

It's that a high investment in both attributes might make too many powerful hybrid capstones simultaneously affordable.

If the only requirements for Tooler, Hybrid Server, and Tricky were raw attribute totals, an athlete could potentially unlock all three from the same investment.

Specific path prerequisites help solve that, but only if pursuing those prerequisites involves meaningful choices or opportunity costs.

If every eligible Trait can be acquired for free once its attribute thresholds are reached, path prerequisites alone may not create sufficient differentiation.

That means we eventually need to confirm exactly how Trait acquisition competes for resources, independently of attribute growth.

My proposed design rule: Allow extensive overlap in capabilities and outcomes, but require distinct investments for distinct specializations. Attribute totals establish eligibility; the specific progression choices establish what kind of athlete emerges.

That preserves the flexibility you're aiming for without reducing archetypes to rigid classes or making every athlete with the same attribute distribution function identically.

Under the proposed model, this athlete has two current Traits: A3 and B2.

A1, A2, and B1 are prerequisites.

The unresolved question is whether prerequisite Traits remain mechanically active after the athlete progresses beyond them.

That distinction has major implications.

Model A: Prerequisites remain active
The athlete retains all five acquired effects, but only two count toward their current Trait capacity.

This rewards deep investment because a single path provides an accumulating package of benefits.

However, it creates a risk that long paths become disproportionately powerful.

Model B: Only current Traits remain active
The athlete benefits from A3 and B2, while earlier Traits function solely as progression requirements.

This strongly limits total active effects and gives capstones substantial importance.

However, it risks making progression feel like replacing one technique with another, rather than developing an increasingly capable athlete.

Model C: Prerequisites are incorporated into descendants
Each later Trait inherits or improves the relevant functions of earlier Traits.

A3 effectively represents the accumulated development of A1 and A2, but only A3 counts as the current Trait.

This seems particularly compatible with the model you're describing.

It preserves progression while making the current Trait a meaningful representation of the athlete's specialization.

I would provisionally favor Model C, although it should not be canonized without examining the existing Traits.

For example, a deep serving Trait could improve earlier serving capabilities without requiring the athlete to maintain several separately counted active Traits.

3. Trait capacity and attribute requirements solve different problems
This gives us three distinct mechanisms for regulating a build:

Mechanism

Controls

Attribute requirements

Which Traits an athlete is eligible to acquire

Branch structure

Which specializations can coexist

Current Trait capacity

How many independent specializations an athlete can maintain

These constraints interact but should not be interchangeable.

An athlete could satisfy every numerical requirement for Hybrid Server while lacking the necessary serving-path prerequisites.

Another athlete could satisfy those prerequisites but be unable to claim Hybrid Server because doing so would exceed their build capacity.

A third could have both the requirements and available capacity, but have already committed to a structurally incompatible branch.

These are three different reasons why a Trait might be unavailable.

4. Tooler and Hybrid Server as a compatibility test
This is an excellent pair to use when designing the eventual topology.

Both require POW and ACC, but they develop different aspects of those attributes.

Tooler combines attacking power, Court Vision, and Broad Range.

Hybrid Server combines POW jump topspin serving and ACC float serving.

Their attribute requirements can overlap almost completely without implying that their paths should overlap.

A useful intended relationship might be:

A full Tooler can acquire some serving improvements.

A full Hybrid Server can acquire some attacking improvements.

Combining their deepest specializations should be exceptionally expensive or impossible.

A partial Tooler/Hybrid Server combination might be viable and strategically interesting.

That final possibility is particularly important. If the tree permits athletes to stop short of capstones and diversify, it creates meaningful intermediate builds rather than forcing everyone to pursue one of a handful of predefined outcomes.

5. One architectural issue: what counts as a path?
Before establishing a numerical Trait capacity, we should define a path precisely.

Consider an athlete who claims two branches sharing three prerequisite nodes.

Does that count as two current Traits? Does claiming one branch prevent the other? Can an athlete return to an earlier junction and begin a second specialization?

I would distinguish:

Node: An individual Trait.

Branch: A sequence of Traits extending from a decision point.

Path: The complete progression from an attribute root to a particular claimed Trait.

Current Trait: The deepest claimed Trait on a path that is not serving as a prerequisite for another claimed Trait.

With that definition, the athlete's build can be understood as a collection of current Traits and their prerequisite paths.

Mathematically, these current Traits form the frontier of the athlete's claimed Trait tree.

That gives us a useful way to express build capacity without treating every previously acquired Trait as an independent slot.

It also means that claiming a deeper Trait along an existing path might not increase the number of current Traits, while beginning a new independent branch would.

That is a powerful distinction:

Depth consumes progression investment; breadth consumes current Trait capacity.

This should be treated as a candidate rule rather than an automatic consequence. If every deeper node is free once prerequisites are met, depth could become too inexpensive. We still need a meaningful acquisition cost or progression gate.

6. What I would establish next
Before assigning individual POW Traits, I think we should answer two architectural questions.

First: Does advancing along a Trait path preserve, replace, or incorporate the effects of its prerequisites?

This determines what deep specialization actually grants.

Second: Are adjacent branches always mutually exclusive, or can athletes claim multiple branches when they have sufficient capacity?

Your description suggests that adjacency and tree structure should determine compatibility, but it does not yet establish whether every fork is exclusive.

My preference is not to make every fork exclusive. Some branches should be mutually exclusive, others should permit expensive combinations, and some should naturally coexist.

That allows the tree to support both meaningful specialization and unusual hybrid builds.

The broader conclusion is that we no longer need to design archetypes as mutually exclusive build packages. We can design the Trait graph so that its reachable combinations produce the intended archetypes, while still permitting unforeseen but legitimate builds.

That is a stronger system than assigning each athlete a single archetype label, because the opportunity costs emerge from the same progression rules that govern every athlete.
